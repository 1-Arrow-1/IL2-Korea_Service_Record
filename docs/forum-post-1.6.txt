IL-2 Korea Service Record 1.6 — where he flew, the hours he logged, the paper that came
with the medal, and a word for the men who did not come back


Gentlemen,

1.6 is out. As before, everything below is new or changed since 1.5; the earlier posts
describe the rest, and none of it has changed.


THE MAP

Every mission now has a map, and it is worth saying plainly what it does and does not show.
The flight log the game keeps is a log of events, not a continuous track — it records the
moment something happened, not a fix every few seconds. So the line you see is the places
the aeroplane is known to have been, joined in the order it was there: the take-off, the
kills, the hits it took, the bail-out if there was one, the landing. Each mark carries the
mission clock, so you can read the sortie as it happened.

Where you used the game's time skip, the jump is drawn as a dashed line rather than a solid
one, because the aeroplane did not fly that stretch and it would be a lie to draw it as if
it had.

Two other things are on it. Your squadron's losses that day are marked with crosses, and
where two men went down in the same place the cross carries a count. And the front line is
drawn as it stood on the day of that mission — the game carries nine dated front lines for
the war, from Operation Rugged in April 1951 to the armistice, and the map picks the one in
force. It moves a great deal over two years, which is the point of drawing it per mission
rather than once. The operations map has a toggle for the losses; the mission map zooms
with the wheel.


THE FLIGHT RECORD

A pilot's hours were not a number in a database, they were a form, and he signed it every
month. So there is now a flight record: AF Form 5, the individual flight record the Air
Force actually kept, one sheet per month, with the grade he held that month, the aircraft,
the hours by day, and the remarks column carrying the mission symbol and what he shot down.
It pages by month and prints one sheet to a page if you want it on paper.

Soviet and North Korean pilots get their own flight book instead, in their own form.

The Form 5 stays in English whatever language you run the tracker in, because it is a form
and the form was printed in English. The page around it speaks your language as usual.


THE CITATION AND THE CERTIFICATE

Every decoration now has the words that came with it.

Click a medal and you get its citation, composed from what the man actually did — for an
award earned on a single sortie, that sortie; for one earned over a period, the period; for
a unit citation, the squadron's work. A second button opens the certificate itself, on a
drawn form transcribed from the originals, and signed by the men who would have signed it
on that date: Truman or Eisenhower, Finletter or Talbott, Vandenberg or Twining, and the
Fifth Air Force commander of the day. Repeat awards are worded as the period worded them —
"is entitled to wear a second bronze Oak Leaf Cluster in addition to the Silver Star
previously awarded" — rather than being given a fresh certificate as if the first had not
happened.

Soviet and North Korean decorations get the наградной лист, the award recommendation sheet,
filled out as the regimental clerk would have filled it: the man's particulars, his service,
what he did, and the commander's conclusion, with the unit's stamp and two signatures. It
stays in Russian, as it would have been, and every line has a translation on hover in
whatever language you are reading.

Unit citations open their certificate too.


CAPTURED

Swoose asked for this one, and he was right to.

When one of your pilots is shot down over enemy territory and survives the crash, the game
marks him as evading, and a day or two later it walks him back to his own lines and returns
him to the squadron — the tab will tell you the date he is due back. Now and then it does
something else and takes a man for good: missing, not killed, gone from the roll. I have
seen that once in some five hundred sorties.

The one man it never does it to is you. The commander always evades, always walks home,
wherever he comes down, and there was no way to say otherwise.

The Career Helper now has a Captured tab. It lists the men who went down over enemy ground
and came through it — the evaders the game is walking back, and anyone who lost his
aircraft on a sortie he survived — and lets you say that this one did not make it back.
You can only do it while that sortie is still his latest, which is to say before he has
flown again.

The game takes it from there, and handles it properly. If it is the commander, the next
day brings up the Commander Captured screen with the usual choices: postpone, end the
career, or assign a new commander.

Whether a man walked out or spent the next two years in a camp was not something the
squadron got to choose. But you do, and a war in which nobody is ever taken prisoner reads
wrongly.


THE MEDALS

All the decorations have been redrawn, and both the artwork and the way it is worn follow
the regulations of the Korean War period rather than modern practice, which differs more
than you would expect.

The devices are sized as the regulations size them, and the difference is visible: an oak
leaf cluster on a full-size medal is 13/32 of an inch, on a ribbon bar 5/16, so the same
cluster is noticeably larger on the medal. A campaign star is 3/16 whatever its metal, so
the silver star that stands for five bronze is the same size as the bronze beside it. Army
and Air Force service stars are worn one point up. The cluster's stem points to the
wearer's right. A Bronze Star's Combat V sits to the wearer's right of any clusters, and
during Korea the V belonged to the Bronze Star alone — the Air Medal and the Commendation
Medal did not get one until later.

Where four clusters must go on a full-size medal they are worn three in a row with the
fourth centred above them, not four across, and a silver campaign star sits on the centre
line with the bronze ones around it, the first to the wearer's right. Ribbons are three to
a row, which is the normal arrangement, going to four only when three would stack
implausibly high. Nothing is ever shrunk to make it fit: a row of four or five medals is
fitted by letting them overlap, with the senior one inboard and fully exposed, because that
is how a rack is actually mounted.

The USAF coat has been redrawn as well, and now wears the officer's rank on the shoulder
loops.
