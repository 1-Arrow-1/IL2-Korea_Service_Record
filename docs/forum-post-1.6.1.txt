IL-2 Korea Service Record 1.6.1 — a decoration that was being given for the wrong thing,
two that were missing, and paperwork that did not say what it meant to say


Gentlemen,

1.6.1 is a maintenance release. No new screen, nothing to learn. It corrects a decoration
that was being awarded for something it was never awarded for, adds the two medals that
gap left behind, slows the unit citations down to something like the real rate, fixes
four faults in the citations and certificates, and stops the Career Helper passing over a
mission you warped through. Everything in 1.6 is unchanged and the earlier posts still
describe it.


THE BRONZE STAR AND ITS "V"

This is the one worth reading.

The Bronze Star Medal can be given for heroism or for meritorious service, and when it is
given for heroism it carries the bronze letter V. What I had not understood is that the
regulations exclude participation in aerial flight from the V. An airman could earn one,
and some did — defending the field, pulling a man out under fire, fighting after coming
down on the wrong side — but never for anything he did in the air. Heroism in the air is
what the Distinguished Flying Cross and the Air Medal are for, and a man does not get both
systems for the same act.

Every rung of the V ladder was firing on exactly the wrong things: one air victory, or
twenty-five targets strafed, or being wounded while still scoring. And the paperwork
repeated the error — the citation read "while serving as pilot of an F-86 ... in action
near Sinuiju" and the certificate said the same, which is precisely the sentence the
regulation forbids.

It is fixed. The award now asks only for some service behind the man and then leaves it to
chance, because the game models no ground combat and there is nothing in a career that can
stand for the deed. The citation and the certificate use the formula the regulations give
for an airman decorated for a non-flying act — "heroism not involving participation in
aerial flight" — in all six languages, and the award no longer draws a narrative from that
day's sortie, because there is no day.

If you already hold one, you keep it. Nothing is taken back.


THE SOLDIER'S MEDAL, AND THE NAVY AND MARINE CORPS MEDAL

Taking the flying out of the V leaves a gap, and the period filled it with a medal of its
own: heroism with no enemy in it at all. A man pulled from a burning aeroplane on the ramp,
a rescue from the sea, a fire fought on the flight line.

The Air Force had nothing of its own for this until the Airman's Medal of 1960, so in Korea
an airman was given the Army's — the Soldier's Medal, authorised by Act of Congress in July
1926. The naval services have their own, the Navy and Marine Corps Medal. Both are in now,
worn after the Distinguished Flying Cross and before the Bronze Star, with their art, their
names and descriptions in six languages, and their own certificate transcribed from a
filled original.

Neither citation mentions flying, for the same reason the V's no longer does.


THE UNIT CITATIONS CAME MUCH TOO FAST

The Distinguished Unit Citation and its clusters were written against the squadron's
running total of targets destroyed, and I had guessed at the numbers. Then I measured them
against a real squadron.

Twenty-five missions of a fighter-bomber outfit working over airfields and supply dumps —
the 12th FBS, April 1951 — came to 1,310 surface kills. Upwards of fifty a mission. The
old ladder started at 600 and put the first cluster at 1,300, so the squadron had the
citation and its cluster inside five weeks of a war that ran three years. The 4th Fighter-
Interceptor Wing, the most decorated Sabre wing there was, was cited twice in the whole
war.

The thresholds are now 3,000 / 8,000 / 14,000 / 21,000. The first citation falls somewhere
near the squadron's fifty-seventh mission instead of its seventeenth, and the third cluster
is a long campaign's work. The air-victory route is untouched at 50 / 110 / 180 / 260 — it
was always about right, and only looked generous because the ground route was firing six
times sooner. Whichever fits your unit is the one that decides. The Navy's Presidential
Unit Citation moves with it.

Again, anything already awarded stays awarded.


THE PAPERWORK

Four faults, three of them found by a second pair of eyes going through the Air Force set
one award at a time.

A repeat award was resolving to the base decoration on every Air Force ladder, so a second
Air Medal produced a certificate for a first one — no device named, no entitlement line.
The cause was a lookup keyed by the family name of the decoration, and the Air Medal exists
in both the Air Force and the naval tables, so one quietly overwrote the other.

On the Distinguished Unit Citation and both naval unit citations the entitlement line was
inserted in the middle of a sentence, leaving the rest of it dangling after a stray "and".
It now closes the sentence properly and lets the order line — "By order of the Secretary of
the Navy" — stay where it belongs, at the foot.

The Navy Unit Commendation's text ran straight through the commission pennant printed on
the blank. The heading now sits above the pennant and "Washington" below it, as the real
form has it.

And a Republic of Korea citation issued to a sailor produced "U.S. United States Naval
Forces".

Every certificate in the program — a hundred and twelve of them, Air Force and naval — was
then measured line by line to confirm that nothing overlaps anything and nothing runs off
the sheet.


FLIGHT TIME

The Career Helper offers you the missions you warped through, so that it can credit you
the hours the game hands your wingmen for nothing. It chose which missions to offer by a
rule of thumb — anything flown in under half the briefed time — and only read your flight
log afterwards, to work out where the jumps had been.

That is the wrong way round, and it showed. Two missions on the same day, both flown to
50.5 per cent of their plan, both passed over: one by twelve seconds, the other by
eighteen. The first had been flown straight through and deserved to be passed over. The
second was warped, and its log said so plainly — two jumps, some twenty-four minutes of
route skipped — and the detector that would have proved it never ran at all.

The log decides now. Any mission that came in under its plan has its log read, and a log
with no jump in it means the mission was flown through and nothing is owed, however far
short of the briefing it came in: a plan is a plan, not a promise. The ratio survives
only as a fallback, for a sortie whose log has gone.


THE NAVAL RACK

Still nothing you can earn, for the same reason as last time: the game has no naval careers
yet. Three corrections to it all the same. The unit ribbon on the right breast was set by
the edge of the pocket rather than its centre and sat visibly inboard. The full-size medals
were drawn a shade small against the Medal of Honor and are now ten per cent larger. And
the gold star on the Medal of Honor's neck pad, which marks a second award, was the wrong
way up — a repeat star is worn with one ray down.


A NOTE FOR ANYONE WRITING THEIR OWN AWARDS

Several of the above are the same mistake in different clothes: I trusted the list of
criteria printed at the foot of the game's own awards.cfg. Some of it is wrong. So the
whole list has now been checked against the career processor itself, variable by variable,
and the result is in the repository as docs/AWARD_CRITERIA.md — one table saying what each
criterion actually measures, and whether it can be used for a squadron award, a personal
award, or neither.

The short version: one documented variable does not exist in the program at all and always
reads as zero, several are available only to squadron awards despite the legend suggesting
otherwise, and several that work are not documented anywhere. If you are writing award
conditions, read that table before you spend an evening wondering why nothing fires.
